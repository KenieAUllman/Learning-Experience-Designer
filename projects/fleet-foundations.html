Flagship Case Study

Fleet Foundations

Turning a growing body of fleet information into a framework new hires could actually use.

Industry Maritime Operations
Audience Newly hired operational employees
Role Instructional Designer
Format Responsive eLearning + custom interactions
Methods Needs analysis, content architecture, practice, assessment
Portfolio Reconstruction

This case study reflects professional work I completed in an employer environment. Proprietary content, identifiable information, branding, and operational details have been removed or recreated. The instructional-design process, methodology, and design decisions reflect my actual work.

01 • Problem

Too many facts. Not enough structure.

The training had grown around individual vessels, destinations, and specifications. New hires could see the information, but they had no simple framework for understanding how it connected.

What should learners understand well enough to use again?

02 • Redesign

Give the details somewhere to go.

I shifted the structure from isolated vessel facts to a reusable sequence: classification, environment, purpose, then application.

Before

  1. 01Vessel
  2. 02Specifications
  3. 03Destination
  4. 04Memorize

Individual facts with no organizing model.

After

  1. 01Classification
  2. 02Environment
  3. 03Purpose
  4. 04Apply the model

A reusable framework for interpreting new information.

Design choices

Three decisions shaped the rebuild.

01

Structure before detail.

Teach the organizing model first so new information has somewhere to fit.

02

Practice the pattern.

Use comparison and classification instead of another round of passive review.

03

Assess reasoning.

Ask learners to apply the model to a new situation, not recite a list.

Operating Environment

The environment creates constraints. Vessel design determines how effectively those constraints can be managed.

Changing depth, narrower channels, inland ports, and seasonal conditions make navigation and draft central considerations.

The Four Questions

Before assigning a vessel, ask what the environment and purpose demand.

01 Can it navigate here?

Depth, channel width, draft, maneuverability.

02 Can it operate here?

Weather, waves, exposure, changing conditions.

03 Can it access the destination?

Port size, docking restrictions, vessel footprint.

04 Can it deliver the intended experience?

Viewing, comfort, access, and purpose.

Check Your Mental Model

Five questions. The mastery target is 80%.

1. A shallow-draft vessel is most likely designed primarily for which constraint?
2. Which combination provides the strongest basis for classifying vessels?
3. A destination has an unusually small harbor. Which consideration becomes most important?
4. A scenic northern route prioritizes wildlife viewing but also faces variable open-water conditions. What is the best first judgment?
5. Why is learning classifications more useful than memorizing every individual vessel?

04 • Measure

Completion isn’t enough.

The useful question is whether learners can recognize the pattern and apply it to something new.

RecognizeIdentify the major distinctions.
ExplainConnect design to operating purpose.
ApplyUse the model in a new scenario.

Takeaway

Teach the pattern, not the inventory.

The biggest improvement came from deciding what learners did not need to memorize.
