Flagship Case Study

Fleet Foundations

Replacing memorization with a scalable mental model.

A reconstructed learning experience showing how I reorganized complex onboarding around classification, operating environment, purpose, and application.

Industry Maritime Operations
Audience Newly hired operational employees
Role Instructional Designer
Format Responsive eLearning + custom interactions
Methods Needs analysis, cognitive load, retrieval practice, application, evaluation
Portfolio Reconstruction

This case study reflects professional work I completed in an employer environment. Proprietary content, identifiable information, branding, and operational details have been removed or recreated. The instructional-design process, methodology, and design decisions reflect my actual work.

01 • Context

The system had grown faster than the learning.

A growing maritime organization needed new hires to understand an increasingly complex fleet before instructor-led onboarding. Existing training had accumulated around individual vessels, destinations, and operational facts.

The content was technically available. The harder question was whether the structure helped learners understand how the information fit together.

Learners could encounter dozens of individual facts without first receiving a conceptual framework for organizing them. That made the training harder to retain, harder to apply, and harder to scale as the fleet changed.

02 • Challenge

More information wasn’t the answer.

The problem was not insufficient content. The existing structure encouraged disconnected memorization. Learners were being asked to remember individual assets and destinations before they had a meaningful way to classify what they were seeing.

The problem wasn’t that learners couldn’t memorize enough. The training gave them nothing meaningful to organize the information around.

04 • Design Decisions

Build the mental model first.

01

Teach the schema before the examples.

Give learners a structure that makes later details easier to place and retrieve.

02

Reduce unnecessary cognitive load.

Prioritize transferable concepts over memorizing every individual asset.

03

Use active retrieval.

Move from passive exposure into classification, comparison, and decision-making.

04

Connect content to actual work.

Frame knowledge around the decisions employees need to make and explain.

05

Design for scalability.

A transferable framework can absorb new assets and operating conditions without rebuilding the learning model from scratch.

Operating Environment

The environment creates constraints. Vessel design determines how effectively those constraints can be managed.

Changing depth, narrower channels, inland ports, and seasonal conditions make navigation and draft central considerations.

The Four Questions

Before assigning a vessel, ask what the environment and purpose demand.

01 Can it navigate here?

Depth, channel width, draft, maneuverability.

02 Can it operate here?

Weather, waves, exposure, changing conditions.

03 Can it access the destination?

Port size, docking restrictions, vessel footprint.

04 Can it deliver the intended experience?

Viewing, comfort, access, and purpose.

Deployment Challenge

Use the framework to make a judgment. Feedback focuses on reasoning, not just a right answer.

Check Your Mental Model

Five questions. The mastery target is 80%.

1. A shallow-draft vessel is most likely designed primarily for which constraint?
2. Which combination provides the strongest basis for classifying vessels?
3. A destination has an unusually small harbor. Which consideration becomes most important?
4. A scenic northern route prioritizes wildlife viewing but also faces variable open-water conditions. What is the best first judgment?
5. Why is learning classifications more useful than memorizing every individual vessel?

06 • Measurement

Completion isn’t the finish line.

80% Mastery target for the reconstructed assessment

The measurement plan extends beyond whether someone opened or completed the course. The goal is to check whether the conceptual model transfers into classification, explanation, and application.

Reaction Was the experience clear, relevant, and usable?
Knowledge Can learners identify the major concepts and distinctions?
Application Can learners use the model in new deployment scenarios?
Observation Do trainers see stronger reasoning and more accurate explanations?
Iteration Where do assessment patterns or trainer feedback show a need to revise the design?

07 • Reflection

What mattered most.

The most important design decision wasn’t choosing an interaction or authoring tool. It was deciding what learners did not need to memorize.

Reorganizing the content around transferable concepts created a more scalable model and gave learners a framework they could continue using as the system changed.

Reconstructing this project for my portfolio also gave me an opportunity to revisit earlier design decisions. I preserved the original instructional architecture while improving accessibility, feedback, responsive behavior, and the degree of judgment required in application activities.
